Next Door

When a data center is proposed, residents usually get one number: its size in megawatts. Next Door turns that number into what it means for the town around it, in homes, gallons, jobs and dollars, before the public hearing.

How

made with claude

I built this with Claude, Anthropic’s AI assistant. I started from a prototype I made in Lovable, then Claude checked each assumption against its source, rewrote the formulas in plain JavaScript with tests, and drew the charts. I picked the subject, steered the work and checked it before publishing. The rule is the same as for Tracewire: nothing goes up unless it was checked against a source someone actually opened.

What

four questions, in everyday terms

You enter a town, its state, the facility’s size and the town’s population, which it can look up from the Census. Next Door estimates four things and shows a range for each. Here’s a hypothetical 300-megawatt data center in Culpeper, Virginia, a town of about 20,800 people:

15×the electricity used by every home in town, about 120,000 homes’ worth (EIA, LBNL)
240Kgallons of cooling water a day, about 89 million-gallon pools a year (LBNL, USGS)
480permanent jobs, after a peak of about 1,500 construction workers (JLARC)

The fourth is the tax break. Most states exempt data center equipment from sales tax. Enter the project’s announced investment, and Next Door estimates the sales tax it won’t pay and divides it across the town’s residents. For a $500 million project in Culpeper, that’s about $870 a person.

Checked

checking every number

The prototype looked finished, but its assumptions weren’t checked. Checking them against the sources changed several results:

  • A unit mix-up. The cooling-water figure was in liters per kilowatt-hour of computer power, not gallons per kilowatt-hour of the whole building. Fixing that, and using LBNL’s figure for how much of its rated power a data center draws on average, cut the water estimate by more than half (LBNL).
  • The wrong states. The list of states with a data center tax exemption was wrong for several states. It now comes from Virginia’s legislative auditors, who counted 34 in 2024 (JLARC).
  • Numbers with no source. Construction workers per megawatt, equipment spending per megawatt and the volume of an Olympic pool (published depths differ) couldn’t be backed up, so they came out. JLARC’s per-facility figures and USGS’s million-gallon pool replaced them.
  • No made-up ranges. Where a source gives one figure, the page says so instead of inventing a range.
Ask

what it can’t tell you

These are planning-level estimates from national and Virginia averages, not predictions for one project. The people proposing it can give the real figures, so the page ends with questions to ask at the hearing:

  • Water. Which cooling system will it use, and where will the water come from?
  • Power. Who pays for new power lines, substations and power plants?
  • Jobs. How many permanent jobs will go to people who already live here?
  • Taxes. What state and local tax breaks has the developer applied for, and for how long?

The tool itself shows every assumption, its range and its source.
