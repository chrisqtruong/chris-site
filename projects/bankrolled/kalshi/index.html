Following the money: Kalshi

Kalshi ads show up every time I open an app. So I followed the money: where it came from, how Kalshi makes it, and what it spends to win over regulators, politicians and young customers.

full research, with charts bankrolled
How

made with claude

I researched and wrote this with Claude, Anthropic’s AI assistant. Claude pulled the public records (lobbying disclosures, campaign filings, court dockets, regulator releases) and the reporting, drafted the text and drew the charts. I picked the subject, steered the work and checked it before publishing. AI can get things wrong, so every number in the full research page links to its source. Facts are as of October 7, 2026.

In

the money in

Kalshi didn’t come from gambling. Its founders, Tarek Mansour and Luana Lopes Lara, met at MIT and worked as quant traders before starting it (Contrary Research). In November 2020 the CFTC licensed Kalshi as an exchange, the first federally regulated one for bets on events (CFTC, Forbes). That license is still the whole business. It’s what Kalshi points to when states say it’s running an illegal sportsbook.

The big money came after sports contracts launched in January 2025 (CDC Gaming). Kalshi has raised about $3.6 billion in all (Clay), from Sequoia, Paradigm, Andreessen Horowitz, Coatue and others, and its valuation went from $2 billion in June 2025 (The Block) to $5 billion in October (The Block), $11 billion in December and $22 billion in May 2026 (Decrypt, Cooley).

One detail stuck with me: Donald Trump Jr. has been a paid strategic adviser since early 2025, and the Financial Times reported, citing unnamed sources, that he was paid about $300,000 in Kalshi stock for it (Covers). Neither he nor Kalshi commented.

Made

how it makes money

Kalshi takes a small fee on almost every trade, and almost all of that fee money comes from sports. The figures below are estimates, since Kalshi is private and doesn’t publish its finances.

89%of Kalshi’s estimated $263.5 million in 2025 fee revenue came from sports (InGame via CDC Gaming)
$40.1Btraded on Kalshi in July 2026 alone, during the World Cup (The Block)
−20%what traders lost on average before fees, in a study of about 314,000 contract prices (VoxEU)

That last number is from economists at University College Dublin. People buying long shots priced at 10 cents or less lost more than 60% (Burgi, Deng and Whelan). It works like a sportsbook, except Kalshi lets you start at 18, while most state sportsbooks make you wait until 21 (CNN).

Out

buying influence

News reports this year said Kalshi first registered to lobby in July 2025 (NOTUS). That’s when it registered its own lobbyists (filing). But federal lobbying records show it hired an outside firm, Rich Feuer Anderson, in December 2021 (filing). Spending has climbed every year since, and the first half of 2026 alone beat all of 2025.

  • Both parties’ insiders. Two former legislative directors to Chuck Schumer (filing), a former Senate Republican floor staffer (filing), former Sen. Blanche Lincoln, who chaired the committee that oversees the CFTC (filing), and the firm of Jeff Miller, finance chair of Trump’s second inaugural committee (filing, OpenSecrets).
  • The founders’ checks. About $1 million to candidates and party committees this election cycle: $614,700 to Republicans and $377,300 to Democrats (NOTUS).
  • The people suing it. In the first half of 2026 Kalshi gave $567,500 to the national associations that back both parties’ governors and attorneys general (OpenSecrets). Attorneys general in several states are suing Kalshi.
  • The states. Registered lobbyists in 41 states by September 2026, nearly all hired since April (OpenSecrets).
Law

the fight, and maryland

At least 17 states are fighting Kalshi in court or with cease-and-desist orders (DeFiRate case tracker). Kalshi pulled its sports contracts out of Nevada in March 2026 after a court order (DeFiRate), and courts in Washington and Michigan ordered it to block users there, with fines of $120,000 and $500,000 a day (GeekWire, Michigan AG).

Federal appeals courts have ruled against Kalshi three times: the Sixth Circuit in Ohio and Tennessee (The Block), and the Ninth Circuit twice, in Nevada and in a case brought by California tribes (Finance Magnates, Buchalter). The Third Circuit ruled for it in New Jersey (Lowenstein), and New Jersey has asked the Supreme Court to settle it (docket 26-299). Meanwhile the CFTC, which fought Kalshi until it dropped its appeal in May 2025 (Finance Magnates), has been suing states on Kalshi’s side since April 2026 (CFTC).

Here in Maryland, the state ordered Kalshi to stop in April 2025 (Next Event Horizon), but agreed to hold off until the federal appeals court rules (Covers). That case was argued in May 2026 and is still waiting on a decision (InGame). In August, Baltimore City sued Kalshi, Robinhood, Webull and Coinbase under its consumer protection law (The Block). Those are allegations for now.

Cost

who pays

No study measures harm among prediction-market users yet. But sports are most of Kalshi’s business, and the research on online sports betting points one way. Peer-reviewed studies link it to lower credit scores and more bankruptcies and delinquencies (Management Science), less saving and more credit card debt for households already short on money (Journal of Financial Economics, via NBER), and more calls to the problem-gambling hotline and more suicides (Information Systems Research).

Health records tell the same story. Diagnosed gambling disorder rose from 3.0 to 4.8 per 100,000 patients between early 2018 and early 2026 in states with legal sports betting, and fell from 3.1 to 2.2 in states without it (Epic Research). That shows a pattern, not proof of cause.

To be fair to the other side: Federal Reserve economists found that Kalshi’s markets give a useful, continuously updated read on what people expect from the Fed and the economy (Diercks, Katz and Wright, NBER). That value comes from the economics and politics markets, though, and most of the money is in sports.
