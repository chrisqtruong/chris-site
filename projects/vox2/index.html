Why

why i built it

I was working on an AI product with a design team, and one of my teammates only spoke Portuguese. Every message between us took a detour: I’d paste mine into Google Translate or an AI app, he’d do the same, then answer in English so I could understand. We were always one browser tab away from actually talking to each other.

I wanted something so light it disappears: a way to break the language barrier in seconds and get back to the conversation. When it first worked, it felt like I’d unlocked another world. That’s the feeling I want Vox2 to give anyone trying to connect with someone across a language.

trust, not just translation

The translation comes back into your language with a score for how much of your meaning survived. It compares meaning, not exact words.

No account, no server, no analytics. Voice, screen snips and the meaning check run on your own computer.

Testing

testing my own feature, honestly

A score is only useful if you can trust it, so I test the meaning check like a researcher would: real sentences in 11 languages, with mistakes planted on purpose (a flipped “not”, a changed number, a swapped pronoun), and fresh sentences every round so it can’t just memorize the test.

96% → 8%of gibberish passed off as a match; now it says “can’t check”, in all 11 languages, without misfiring on any of 6,160 good translations

Five test runs and two targeted tests, 11 languages, more than 10,000 translations scored. Every run is published with its method and raw results on GitHub.

The first test caught under a third of the mistakes, so I added targeted checks for negations, numbers, pronouns and opposites, and taught it to say “can’t check” when text never got translated, instead of showing a misleading number.

Then I ran it on fresh sentences nobody had looked at, and errors caught dropped from 79% to 70%. That’s the more honest number. The earlier sentences had been read while tuning the checks, so they flattered it, like grading a student on the practice questions.

70% sounds low, but it isn’t alarming. Every bad sentence in the test hides a mistake on purpose, and some can’t show up in a round trip at all, like a “he” passing through a language that doesn’t mark gender. The mistakes that change what you meant are caught almost every time: a dropped part of a sentence 98%, a changed number 94%, a missing “not” 85%. The misses are mostly opposite words and swapped pronouns, which need a second opinion to see. Without the checks, the score alone caught 35%. And when Vox2 can’t tell, it says so instead of guessing.

The goal behind all of it: when you write to your family, your in-laws or a coworker in their language, you should know they’ll understand what you meant. Language shouldn’t decide who feels close to you, or what you get to read.

Next

where the testing goes next

The plan is phases, not a big rewrite. Each one is measured on sentences the checks have never seen, and every result gets published, including the ones that don’t move the number.

  1. More engines, not just Google. Today every test uses Google for everything, so one engine is grading its own work. The next runs bring in DeepL (a German translation service that many professional translators prefer for natural-sounding results), Microsoft Translator, Apple’s on-device translator and an AI engine, both to translate and to translate back, plus native speakers spot-checking a sample, starting with Vietnamese.
  2. Beyond English. The checks look hardest at the English side today. Next, the same checks in other languages, so a reply in Vietnamese or Portuguese gets the same scrutiny.
  3. Show everything that changed. When a check flags a problem, Vox2 already underlines the words behind it and lists the ones that didn’t come back. Next: highlight words that were added too, and test whether the highlights actually help people catch mistakes.
  4. A second opinion. Translate back with a different engine, so one engine can’t agree with its own mistake, plus an optional AI double-check for the messages that matter most.
  5. Measure it against the research. Run Vox2’s checks on the WMT test sets, the yearly benchmark where research systems are scored against mistakes marked by professional translators, and publish how it compares to systems like xCOMET and CometKiwi.
  6. Another fresh test. Confirm today’s numbers on a second set of sentences nobody has read, so they aren’t tuned to the last one.
  7. Harder, more realistic tests. Messy text from real life (chat messages, screen snips, names, slang) and the cases it still misses, like pronouns, tested on purpose until they hold up.

All of it has to stay light. The checks run on your own computer, and anything heavier, like the AI double-check, stays optional. The bar isn’t more features. It’s trusting a translation enough to hit send without a second app open.

Ahead

where vox2 is heading

The core stays tiny: a window you call up with a shortcut. Bigger things come as add-ons you turn on when you need them, and they unload when you’re done.

live captions for calls and videos

On a Zoom call or a YouTube video, Vox2 listens to the computer’s audio and shows translated captions as people speak, with whole sentences and context instead of word-by-word subtitles.

Every paragraph gets the meaning check, so you know where to look twice instead of trusting one giant block.
