Vox2

A tiny two-way translator for your desktop. Type, paste, speak or snip text in any app, and read it, or hear it, in another language.

Why

a teammate who spoke portuguese

A while ago I was on a design team with someone who only spoke Portuguese. Every message went through a translator first. I’d paste mine into Google Translate or an AI app, he’d do the same and reply in English, and we were always a browser tab away from actually talking.

I wanted something faster than that tab, that also tells you whether your meaning made it across. Vox2 is a small window you open with a shortcut from any app. The first time it worked, it felt like I’d unlocked another world.

Process

how I worked

I picked up this way of working at Accenture’s Houston Innovation Hub, running design thinking workshops for energy and utility companies. Start from a real problem, design before you build, test with evidence, and keep going until it holds up. Vox2 went through the same steps, with a team of one and Claude Code. Most of that work is a study in usability: how few steps it takes to get a translation, and whether your hands ever have to leave the keyboard.

Design

the small details

A translator you open twenty times a day has to stay out of the way, so I weigh most choices in keystrokes. Tap the right Ctrl key to dictate (the right Option key on a Mac), or hold it to talk. Press Esc in any app to stop a reading. In the bubble, Tab to the language and type “spa” for Spanish.

words settle in

New words show in gray until the translation settles, so the text doesn’t jump around while you type.

a score for your meaning

The translation is translated back into your language, with a score for how much of your meaning made it. Different wording that says the same thing still scores well.

37 themes

People care about color, so there are a lot of themes, and you can star your favorites to keep them on top. Scores can also use a colorblind-safe palette with ✓, ! and ✕, so nothing relies on color alone.

from any app

Select text anywhere and press a key (on a Mac, just tap the right ⌘). The translation shows up in a bubble by your cursor, with its score. Click your text to see all of it, or drag the bubble aside and it stays there like a sticky note.

room to read

One shortcut fits the window to your text. Press it again for a wider reading width, again for a compact window that scrolls, and once more to fit again.

private by default

No account, no server, no analytics. Your text goes only to the translation engine you pick, and voice, screen snips and the meaning check run on your own computer.

Testing

does the score mean anything?

A score is only useful if you can trust it. So I test the meaning check on real sentences in 11 languages, with mistakes planted on purpose (a flipped “not”, a changed number, a swapped pronoun), and new sentences every round so it can’t memorize the answers.

35% → 70%of meaning errors caught on sentences it had never seen, from the score alone to now. Goal: 90%+

96% → 8%of gibberish passed off as a match; now it says “can’t check”, in all 11 languages, without misfiring on any of 6,160 good translations

Seven test runs and several targeted tests, 11 languages, more than 15,000 translations scored. Every run is published with its method and raw results on GitHub.

The first version caught under a third of the mistakes. I added checks for negations, numbers, pronouns and opposites, and taught it to say “can’t check” instead of guessing. On sentences nobody had seen, it now catches 70%. Earlier runs said 79%, but I’d read those sentences while tuning the checks, which is a bit like grading yourself on the practice test. So I go with 70%.

Then I tried harder text: real medicine labels and legal notices, where a wrong dose or “unless” instead of “if” can hurt someone. It caught about half. I added a check for units and taught it that “unless” means “if not”, and on 144 new sentences it now catches 71%. That still isn’t good enough to call it a safety feature, so I don’t.

What I’m after is simple. When you write to your family, your in-laws or a coworker in their language, you should know they’ll understand you.

Next

what’s next

Vox2 is my closest study in usability: can someone pick it up without instructions, and does it stay quick enough to use every day? So far I’ve mostly answered that by using it myself. Next is watching other people use it.

The app itself stays small. Bigger features will be add-ons you can turn on when you need them, and every change to the meaning check still gets tested and published.

  1. Real people, real feedback. Hand it to family, friends and coworkers, watch where they get stuck, and build from what they actually do.
  2. Quicker, and yours. More shortcuts and settings, so you can shape Vox2 around how you work and get a translation without reaching for the mouse.
  3. A second opinion. Translate back with a different engine, so one engine can’t agree with its own mistake, plus an optional AI double-check for the messages that matter most. That’s where the remaining misses are.
  4. Measured against the research. Run the checks on the WMT test sets, where research systems are scored against mistakes marked by professional translators, and publish how Vox2 compares.

live captions for calls and videos

On a Zoom call or a YouTube video, Vox2 listens to the computer’s audio and shows translated captions as people speak, with whole sentences and context instead of word-by-word subtitles.

Words settle as each sentence ends; older lines slide up into a transcript you can scroll back through.

drop in a whole document

Drag a paper or report onto one side, and the translation streams into the other, paragraph by paragraph, with the two sides scrolling together. Copy it out with the headings, lists and tables intact.

Every paragraph gets the meaning check, so you know where to look twice instead of trusting one giant block.

Whatever I add, it has to stay light enough to open without thinking about it.
