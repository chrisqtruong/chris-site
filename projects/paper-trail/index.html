Why

keep the record in one place

I’m constantly impressed by independent projects that keep a public record of corruption, corporate greed and abuse of power, built by people who decided someone should be keeping track. One that stuck with me is Rachel LaPointe’s Gendered Police Violence, where a New York social worker documents gender-based violence by police, case by case. Work like that inspired me to start my own.

I want a fairer, more equitable world, and getting there means watching what large companies do with their money and power. The trouble is that the record is scattered: a campaign filing here, a court ruling there, a fine announced on a Friday afternoon. Powerful institutions count on it staying that way, easy to miss and easy to forget.

Paper Trail is my attempt at using AI for public good: gather what’s already public, check it against primary sources, and keep it in one searchable, permanent place that anyone can read and reuse.

How

from headline to record

The framework is a pipeline, and none of it needs anyone to press a button.

  1. Gather. A small program polls public feeds every few minutes (regulators, courts, government filings, newsrooms) and posts what it finds as unverified headlines, clearly labeled.
  2. Verify. Every hour, a scheduled Claude run reads primary sources first, opens each candidate at its source, and labels it confirmed, reported or disputed. Only confirmed items enter the ledger of who paid whom.
  3. Keep. Every source link gets a saved copy on the Internet Archive, older posts move to a monthly archive, and every change is committed to GitHub. Nothing is ever deleted.
  4. Show. One fast static page per tracker: a feed, the ledger, charts, entities, comments, and a status page that says what was checked and what couldn’t be read.

Tracewire runs all four steps. Bankrolled, the first tracker, came before the live headline feed and saved copies; it gathers, verifies, archives and shows through its hourly run.

Rules

honest about what it knows

The trackers take a side on what matters, so the facts have to be airtight. The same standards apply to every one of them.

  • Three labels. Confirmed, reported or disputed, on every item, so you always know how sure it is.
  • Accused until convicted. Lawsuits are allegations; it never calls anything corruption in its own voice, only what was filed, charged or reported, and by whom.
  • No shaky numbers. When sources disagree on a figure, it’s left out.
  • Mistakes stay visible. Corrections are made in place, late finds keep their true date, and each tracker publishes its own blind spots.

Yours

start your own

The framework ships as the Tracker Kit: one command turns a short config into a complete tracker (the page, its data files, the source checklist and the hourly AI instructions), ready to put on GitHub. It comes with starting points for corporate lobbying, environmental disasters and conflict accountability.

Each tracker lives in its own repository with its own data, comments and schedule, so a mistake in one can never leak into another, and any of them can be handed to someone else. A tracker’s behavior is defined by plain text files it rereads every run, so improving one means editing a file, not rewriting a program.
